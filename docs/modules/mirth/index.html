Overview

Mirth runs neural amp modelling captures on a Daisy patch.Init(). It comes in two builds: Mirth Lite, for an unmodified patch.Init(), and Mirth, which adds a small screen (see Hardware).

A capture is a small neural network trained to behave like one particular amp. For Mirth we use NAM’s A2-Lite neural network: 23 layers, each a dilated causal convolution with a LeakyReLU activation, joined by residual and skip connections — 1,871 parameters in all. Small enough for a Eurorack module, and a format TONE3000’s library lets you filter for.

The first bank, AMPS, plays twelve of those captures. The second, NOT-AMPS, takes the same amps but messes with the parameters to create effects that are definitely not real amps. A third bank, FILTERS, has its own page: Mirth (filters).

Named — like everything here — after the nursery rhyme, in the version that runs one for sorrow, two for mirth. Not affiliated with, or endorsed by, Neural Amp Modeler, TONE3000, Electrosmith, or the makers of the amps and pedals captured. Product names describe what was captured.

An amp as a patch

So what actually is an amp and a speaker, as a patch? Take it apart, and there are only a few kinds of module in there. The tone controls, and the capacitors between the stages, are filters. The valves are clippers: close to linear around the middle, then they flatten at the extremes, and not evenly, as if there is an offset going in. On a big note, many amps pull their own power supply down, and that is a compressor. And the speaker is a cone on a spring, so it has a resonance: hit it and it rings on. That is a resonant band-pass filter, with some more clipping when it reaches the end of its travel. Some captures include the speaker, and some are the amp alone.

Why a network can copy it

Each neuron reads the current sample of audio and some earlier ones. That reaching back in time is a delay, and by the last layer it reaches about 132 milliseconds, long enough to catch a speaker ringing on and an amp recovering from a big note. Each of those samples is multiplied by a weight and they are added up, and the weights are what make a filter.

Put the delay and the filter together and some frequencies cancel while others are amplified. With enough of them lined up at one frequency, it keeps going after the input stops. That is the resonance, the ringing.

Then the activation squashes the values, flattening anything below zero almost to nothing, and that is what copies the clipping. A fixed amount added just before it, called the bias, moves where the squashing starts, which is where the unevenness comes from.

An amp and a cab have many elements that affect the sound, so the network has to be big enough to capture the nuance. A2-Lite has 23 layers, three lanes side by side, and 1,871 parameters, the weights and biases: big enough for that, and small enough for a Eurorack module. Training plays the same signal through the real amp and the network, and adjusts those numbers until the two match.

What a capture cannot do

The network only sees the last 132 milliseconds, so anything slower cannot be in a capture. It learns one setting of the amp, which is why a capture has no knobs. And amps flatten, they do not fold: turn every neuron into a wavefolder, and it stops sounding like an amp. Which is where the second bank starts.

The not-amps

Nineteen not-amps: fifteen built from the starter captures, four from random numbers. Each is changed in one way while it plays, with one control, STEER, that you can turn or patch. They were chosen by measurement first: every candidate was compared with 236 real amp captures, by audio descriptors and by a neural model of how things sound, and only those that leave the region where amps sit were kept — then by ear.

Controls

The knobs and jacks are the same on both builds. Each knob is summed with the CV jack under it.

CV_3 (+ CV_7) — —
CV_4 (+ CV_8) DRIVE into the network, −20 to +20 dB, unity at noon DRIVE
B7 short the next patch in the bank
B7 held 1.5 s the next bank: AMPS → NOT-AMPS → FILTERS

Audio in on IN_L; the same mixed signal on both outputs. MIX fully down is the dry input alone, for comparing. DRIVE changes the sound far more than the level: most of the captures grow more harmonics as it rises while their output hardly moves. Each bank remembers its place, so going round the banks comes back to where you were. In FILTERS, knobs 2 and 3 are FREQ and RES: see Mirth (filters).

Hardware

Bill of materials: Mirth Lite runs on Daisy patch.Init(). For full Mirth add a 64×48 SSD1306 OLED (I²C) screen · a few hook-up wires · and a printed panel. The same screen and wiring as Joy: replacing the B8 toggle with the OLED for more feedback during use.

Build your own patch.Init()

The patch.Init() is often out of stock, but it is easy to build your own. Daisy Audio publishes the design on its patch.Init() page: one download holds the control board’s Gerbers and KiCad files, a PCB front panel and a parts list. Order the boards, add a Daisy Patch Submodule, and the rest is easy to get: 13 jacks, 4 pots, a button, a toggle, an LED and a microSD socket. My Joy is built this way, with a printed panel.

Two things to watch on the parts list: it shows one 2×5 socket, but the board takes four, and it doesn’t include knobs.

More captures

Download A2 captures from TONE3000 — filter by architecture — and copy the .nam file to the root of the card. At the next boot Mirth converts it: it takes the A2-Lite network, names it from the file, levels it with the rest and writes the capture beside it, about three seconds a file, then moves the .nam into nam_done, or into nam_refused with the reason on the screen if it is not an A2 capture. tools/nam_to_a2nb.py in the firmware repo does the same on a computer. Up to 32 on a card. Most captures on TONE3000 are licensed for your own use, so they can go on your card but not be passed on.

Updating from v1.0.0

  • Replace the firmware with v1.1.0’s .bin.
  • Replace the twelve starter captures with the zip’s: the same networks, now levelled by A-weighted loudness rather than RMS (v1.0.0’s SVT played about 2 dB quiet, its Sun Bender 4 dB loud).
  • Copy the notamps folder to the card: without it the four RND not-amps and MUTATE KAY show N need cap.
  • The knobs have moved. Knob 1 is MIX, 2 STEER, 4 DRIVE; B7 now steps through the patches, so a patch can no longer be chosen by CV, and there is no bypass (MIX fully down is the dry input). On Mirth Lite the B8 toggle no longer picks the bank: hold B7. FRZ E TR has gone, and the not-amps have new numbers.

Flash it — no compiler needed

Mirth runs from the Daisy's QSPI chip through the Daisy bootloader: a one-time bootloader install, then the firmware goes on with an SD card. The same card then holds the captures. No compiler needed.

Read this first — The card must be one FAT32 partition of 2 GB or less, with the rest of the card left unallocated. A full-size FAT32 volume on a big card fails at start-up, and exFAT is not read at all.

The firmware is for a patch.Init() with the OLED fitted; Mirth Lite is for a stock patch.Init(), no screen. Both flash the same way and both take the same card: the zip holds the twelve starter captures, the five files the not-amps need in `notamps`, the seven filters, their credits, and these card instructions. Coming from v1.0.0? See "Updating from v1.0.0" below.

One time per module — install the bootloader

  1. Plug the Daisy Patch.Init in with a USB-C data cable.
  2. Hold BOOT, tap RESET, then release BOOT.
  3. Open the Daisy Web Programmer and go to its Bootloader section.
  4. Click Flash. That is the bootloader on — you never have to do this again.

Every time — load the firmware

  1. Format the card: one FAT32 partition of 2 GB or less. On a Mac, check the disk number with diskutil list external first — this erases it — then diskutil partitionDisk /dev/diskN MBR "MS-DOS FAT32" DAISY 2G "Free Space" REST R.
  2. Copy the Mirth or Mirth Lite .bin above to the root of the card. It must be the only .bin on the card.
  3. Unzip the card files and copy everything in the folder to the root as well, the notamps folder included. The not-amps are built from these files, by name.
  4. Insert the card and power-cycle. The bootloader flashes the firmware if it differs from what is installed, and boots.
On a Mac, Finder writes a hidden twin of every file — ._mirth.bin, ._n02_ORANGE_TH.a2nb. The .bin twin confuses the bootloader and the capture twins fail their checksum. After copying, delete them in Terminal with rm -f /Volumes/DAISY/._* /Volumes/DAISY/notamps/._* — the usual cp -X no longer prevents them on current macOS.
